What I Learned About Observability at AWS Summit NYC 2026

July 11, 2026   

Going into the AWS Summit in NYC last month, I knew there would be a lot of talk about agentic AI, but I was still surprised just how much it shaped every single presentation and conversation there was all day long. Soon after I arrived at the convention center, I found myself playing a little game on the exhibition floor. Could I find any vendor that did not have the keywords “agentic” or “AI” in their byline? It was hard. A real needle in a haystack situation. Everyone is obsessed right now. It’s almost like there’s nothing else going on in technology worth talking about. If you’re not selling an AI agent in 2026, or some tool for building them, what are you even doing?

In this brief write-up of my experience at the summit, I want to highlight some things I learned about a more subtle theme of the day: the deepening challenges of observability. A bit buried just beneath all the hype around agentic AI, this to me was the real story of the summit. As we build ever-more complex software systems with ever-greater speed and scale, how are we keeping track of what’s working and what’s broken?

One talk near the end of the day described an “observability paradox”, or a set of inter-related problems confronted by many AWS customers: “more dashboards than decisions, more tools than teammates, more alerts than anyone reads”. I found this framing of the problem deeply relatable. So what is the solution? I heard the phrase “single pane of glass” repeated throughout the day, and this vision always seems to come up in discussions of observability. While most sophisticated observability tools promise this, I have the impression that few, if any, organizations actually rely completely on any single observability tool. I am convinced that at least for the immediate future, the observability paradox will demand significant mental effort by human engineers.

Of course, like every other problem right now, people are looking to agentic AI for solutions. As one speaker observed, “everybody has an SRE agent now”. This is a category of products I hadn’t looked into before the summit, but I learned about several that day. AWS has its own AWS DevOps agent and many of the observability tools that it has out-of-the-box integrations for, such as Datadog and New Relic, have added complementary agentic capabilities. I saw a talk describing how the AWS DevOps Agent and New Relic can coordinate automated incident responses. I also watched an impressive demo from Resolve, a platform dedicated to orchestrating DevOps-related agents and also geared for automated incident response. (Resolve’s yellow “Bag More Nines” tote bags were also a massive hit on the exhibition floor.) While I did not get a clear sense of what these tools cost, the general pricing model seems to be per interaction rather than per token, and I learned that enterprise customers get free credits for AWS DevOps Agent.

I will be interested to explore deeper into what these agents can do, but I’m skeptical of magic bullets. One conversation stood out: a Vistaprint engineer described his team’s observability journey as an iterative process, methodically moving forward one step at a time. They found it effective to focus first on tackling a reduction in their mean time to detection (MTTD) before moving on to concentrate on mean time to resolution (MTTR) and then finally to setting service level objectives (SLOs). This MTTD → MTTR → SLO pathway seems like a sequence worth replicating. It was also clear that while Vistaprint found agentic AI to be a useful tool along the way, it was not a substitute for a coordinated and human-led team effort.

On my train ride home from the summit, I read an interesting but depressing piece from Pragmatic Engineer about the apparent implosion at Meta. In a bout of what has been described as “AI-induced psychosis” the company is undermining what was once its most fundamental strength: a company culture where engineers felt valued and supported to advance their craft. While I did not see clear examples of this mistake at the summit, the danger felt present. There is no question that software companies will need to continue adopting and advancing agentic AI to be competitive in the coming years. Everyone is clearly attuned to that reality. What is less immediately obvious, though on many people’s minds, is how humans can work better with one another to actually understand, support and maintain what we’re building with these exciting new tools.
